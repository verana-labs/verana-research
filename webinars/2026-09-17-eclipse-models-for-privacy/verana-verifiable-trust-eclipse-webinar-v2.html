Why AI agents must have their own identity?

Verifiable Trust: services, organizations and AI agents proving who they are, with no central observer.

Open on the announced question and promise an answer in three parts: what agent identity must prove, why the obvious central fix fails on privacy, and how Verifiable Trust does it in a decentralized way. 90-minute plan: Fabrice slides 1 to 12 (about 40 min), checkpoint questions, Ariel demo plus slide 13 (about 15 min), Fabrice slides 14 and 15, then open discussion.
The Agentic Inflection

AI agents already act on our behalf.
None of them can prove who they are.

Agents initiate connections, call tools and exchange personal data with no human in the loop. The counterparty's only signals today: a domain name and a padlock. That is transport security, not identity.

No human in the loop

Every connection needs an answer at machine speed: who is this, and should data flow? Nobody is there to squint at the URL.

Machine-speed spoofing

Agents can be impersonated, and can be deceived, at a scale and speed no human review process can catch.

Accountability gap

Nothing binds an HTTPS endpoint, an MCP tool or an A2A peer to a legal entity that answers for it.

Your GenAI threat models ask the right question: which organization controls this agent?
Direct bridge to this group's June 2026 webinar on AI tools and privacy by design, and to the LINDDUN GenAI threat framework. The claim: their threat models already identify the right risks; what is missing is a verifiable answer at connection time.
The Answer

An agent's identity must prove three things. Its user's identity proves none of them.

Borrowed identity does not transfer. An agent is not its user, and a platform account is not accountability. An agent needs its own verifiable identity, carrying three provable statements:

01 · Who it is

A DID the agent controls: resolvable and verifiable by anyone, on any transport.

02 · Who operates it

A verifiable credential binding it to the legal entity that answers for it.

03 · What it may do

Ecosystem accreditations scoping its roles and authorizations.

All three: verifiable by any counterparty, before data flows.
This slide is the talk title answered in one screen, and the card on the right is the object to remember: identifier on top, the operator credential in the middle, accreditation stamps at the bottom. Delegation is the key argument: an agent carries a person's authority into hops that person never sees, so the agent itself must be identifiable, bound to an accountable operator, and scoped by accreditations. Ariel's demo later shows this same card as live protocol.
The Trap

The obvious fix, a central agent registry, makes privacy worse.

If one platform vouches for every agent, that platform observes every interaction. In LINDDUN terms: linking, detecting, data disclosure, built into the architecture.

The requirement: agent identity that is verifiable by anyone and observed by no one. That is a decentralization requirement, not a product preference.
Walk the picture: on the left every trust check routes through the hub, so the hub accumulates the relationship graph, and the three LINDDUN threats are properties of the architecture, not implementation bugs. On the right the same actors verify each other directly with Proof-of-Trust on each edge; there is no node that could observe, breach or monetize the graph. The band states the conclusion: decentralization is a requirement.

One Layer

Agent identity is not a special case. It is the same problem, again.

Today each sector rebuilds the solid part too: payment networks run agent directories, education runs credential registries, health runs provider directories, eIDAS runs trusted lists. N sectors × M platforms = incompatible silos.
The differences are models. The infrastructure should be one layer.
The thesis picture: actors on top all reduce to the same three statements; the dashed strip is the only thing that legitimately differs per sector, and it is a model: schemas, roles, governance rules; the solid bar is common infrastructure. A health ecosystem and a payments ecosystem should differ in the dashed strip, not own separate copies of the solid bar. This reframes the landscape slide: those projects each rebuild the solid bar for one silo.
What Verana Is

Verifiable Trust: verify before you connect.

Open, decentralized trust infrastructure. Ecosystems publish their rules, participants prove compliance with verifiable credentials, and anyone can check them without asking a central authority.

01 · Trust Ecosystems

Rules, published

Machine-readable governance: credential schemas, participant roles, who is accredited to issue or verify what.

02 · Verifiable Trust

Proof, not reputation

Peers present DIDs and credentials, and run Proof-of-Trust in both directions before any data flows.

03 · Trust Graph

Discovery, ranked by trust

Find services and agents by the credentials they hold, through APIs or MCP.

Open source, Apache 2.0 · Specification: verana-labs.github.io/verifiable-trust-spec
The one-layer thesis lands here: this is that layer, and the three parts form a cycle. Ecosystems govern what counts as proof; peers prove it to each other at connection time; the trust graph makes the results discoverable, which attracts the next participants into the ecosystems. Everything is specified in the open.
01 · Trust Ecosystems

Ecosystems make trust governable.

An ecosystem is a community that defines what trust means in its domain. Anyone can found one: a sector body, a company network, a government program.

Governance Framework

Anchored to the ecosystem DID: versioned, resolvable, public.

Credential Schemas

JSON Schemas on the registry: what each credential must contain.

Participants

The participant tree: accreditations to issue and to verify, granted, delegated, revoked in public.

Governance becomes data: published, resolvable, enforceable.
The diagram is the website's canonical participant tree, per credential schema, four tiers: the ecosystem root of trust accredits issuer and verifier grantors (T1), grantors accredit the issuers and verifiers that operate (T2), issuers issue credentials to holders and verifiers verify them (T3: humans, services, AI agents, connected objects). Every edge is an accreditation recorded in public: granted, delegated, revocable. Stress the founding point: ecosystems are permissionless to create, which is how sector rules stay sector-owned on shared infrastructure.
02 · Verifiable Trust

Trust resolution runs on the client. Nobody watches you verify.

The privacy property: every input is either public registry data or presented by the peers themselves. Trust becomes a local computation over public models, on both sides.
Walk the sequence and stress that Proof-of-Trust is mutual: peer B verifies peer A exactly as peer A verifies peer B; resolve each other, verify locally on both sides, each checks the other's accreditations against public replicated state, then connect; once the trusted channel exists, peers can optionally exchange private credentials over it (Proof-of-Trust itself uses only public ones). The crossed-out eye is the point for this audience: unlike OCSP checks or platform trust APIs, no third party learns who verified whom, or when. All inputs are public or peer-presented, so each verdict is a local computation, which is the direct answer to the trap slide's linking and detecting threats.
03 · Trust Graph

Trust, once proven, becomes searchable.

Every DID, credential, accreditation and ecosystem on the registry forms a public graph, ranked by verifiable trust instead of SEO.

Query by proof

Find services and agents by credentials held and who accredited them.

Ranked by trust

Verifiable trust signals from the graph, not popularity or ad spend.

For humans and machines

APIs and MCP: agents resolve and choose counterparties before connecting.

Discovery is the demand side of trust: it is why ecosystems compound.
Third pillar: the graph is what makes proven trust useful beyond one connection. Walk the query: it enters at the Payments Ecosystem, follows the public accreditation of a verifier for the Payment Mandate schema, and returns one trust-ranked answer, the same agent whose Proof-of-Trust card appears two slides ahead. Anti-SEO point lands well here: today discovery is rented from platforms and ranking; in the trust graph it is earned through verifiable accreditations. Mention MCP: agents run these queries themselves.
Privacy Engineering

Privacy by design, applied to trust itself.

Unobservability

Trust checks are local. No central broker learns who connected to whom, or when. The relationship graph is not collected anywhere.

Data minimization

Verifiable presentations disclose only the claims a governance rule requires. Selective disclosure is a protocol feature, not a policy promise.

No personal data on ledger

The public registry holds ecosystem metadata only: service DIDs, schemas, accreditations. Personal data stays in wallets, off-ledger, erasable.

Decentralized accountability

Issuers and verifiers are accountable through public, auditable accreditations, not through surveillance of the people who rely on them.

Proactive, not remedial: the protocol enforces these properties before data flows, rather than compensating after a breach. Privacy as the default setting, embedded into design.
This slide answers the two objections privacy engineers raise about anything ledger-based. One, GDPR versus immutability: nothing personal is anchored, only organization-level ecosystem metadata, so erasure rights are not in conflict. Two, surveillance: the design removes the observer instead of regulating it. The closing band deliberately echoes the foundational privacy-by-design principles.
Models As Universal Language

Ecosystem governance is a machine-readable model.

The three parts you just saw are all the same thing underneath: models. This group's founding idea, models as the shared language of privacy engineering, is exactly how Verana encodes trust.

Ecosystem Governance FrameworkResolvable governance document, anchored to the ecosystem DID and versioned.

Participant treeWho is accredited to issue, verify or hold each credential type: delegated and revocable.

Open opportunity: align credential schemas and ecosystem roles with the W3C Data Protection Vocabulary, so purposes, legal roles and processing categories become first-class, queryable model elements.
Quote their mission back to them: the group exists to exchange model-based methods, and Verana governance is exactly that, formal resolvable models that machines evaluate. This is also where the one-layer argument closes: sector differences live in these models, on shared infrastructure. The DPV alignment is a genuine, scoped collaboration proposal: Harshvardhan Pandit presented DPV to this group in 2024, so the thread already exists here.

Verana × Trialog

Normative requirements deserve a conformance tester.

The Verifiable Trust spec defines testable requirements: trust resolution before endpoint use, issuer and verifier authorization, connection acceptance rules. Ontology-driven constraint testing can verify that implementations respect them, and detect when they do not.

Conformance

Does a Verifiable Service or Verifiable User Agent satisfy the applicable normative requirements? Pass, fail, evidence trace.

Misbehavior detection

Endpoint used before trust resolution. Authorization check missing. Interaction continuing after a failed verification.

Shared ontology

A Verifiable Trust ontology, in the spirit of BITO, turns spec requirements into SHACL-testable semantic and behavioral constraints.

We are exploring exactly this with Trialog's ODC-Tester.
Trialog is not presenting the ODC-Tester in this webinar, so this slide introduces the collaboration rather than handing off: Trialog's ontology-driven constraint tester (semantic and behavioral interoperability testing, SHACL over reference ontologies) applied to Verifiable Trust conformance. Credit Tareq Chy and Antonio Kung, and point anyone interested to Trialog's semantic interoperability work.
